caravel (KARE-uh-vel) — a small, fast sailing ship built by the Portuguese that could sail closer into the wind than older ships.

astrolabe (AS-truh-layb) — a tool sailors used to find their ship's position by studying the stars.

Reconquista (ray-kon-KEE-stah) — the centuries-long effort by Christian kingdoms in Spain to take back land ruled by Muslim kingdoms, finished in January 1492.

📖 Today's Focus
The single biggest thing that happened in the Caribbean after 1492 was not planned by anyone.

Columbus did not find what he was looking for. Instead, he found islands full of people. He called them Taíno and Lucayan. These were not the peoples of Asia he expected to meet. He expected people dressed in silk. He expected gold-covered cities. The Taíno and Lucayan lived a different life. They fished. They farmed corn and cassava. They traded goods between neighboring islands. Their world was real. It was organized. It just was not the world Columbus had crossed an ocean to find. What Columbus did next is the subject of this lesson. So is what happened because he came, whether he planned it or not.

Expectation and Reality

Look again at Columbus's letter, which you read yesterday. He said the islands were full of people "without number." He said there was gold "in great quantity." He thought the people he met would be easy to convert. He thought they would be easy to rule too. Why? He said they seemed "timorous," or fearful. He said they had no iron weapons.

Now compare those claims to what we actually know today. Later writers filled in facts Columbus left out. The Taíno grew crops like cassava as their main food. They farmed it carefully across many small plots. They lived in organized towns. Local towns had their own local leaders (caciques). Communities traded goods regularly between islands. None of that sounds like a fearful, disorganized people. None of that sounds like people waiting to be easily conquered.

Was Columbus lying on purpose, then? Probably not. He most likely believed much of what he wrote. But he also badly needed proof. He needed proof that his risky, expensive voyage was worth the money Spain had spent. He found that proof in his letter. It did not matter whether the letter told the whole truth. Keep this lesson with you all year. A primary source tells you what its writer believed. It also tells you what its writer wanted readers to believe. Those two things are not always the same as what actually happened.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

cacique (kah-SEE-kay) — a Taíno word for a local chief or leader.

⚠️ Watch Out: A Common Mistake
It is easy to think everything that happened to Native peoples after 1492 was one single planned event. Do not make that mistake here. The population loss you are about to read about was not a plan anyone wrote down or ordered. Later in this course, you will study times when violence and forced moves were deliberate policy. Those cases deserve to be judged on their own terms. Mixing the two together hides the real cause behind each one.

The Disaster Nobody Planned

Within a hundred years of 1492, the Native population of the Caribbean fell by a huge amount. Whole island communities had existed for centuries. Within a few generations, most of their people were gone. In time, most of the Americas saw this same kind of loss. Most historians agree on this. The large majority of people alive before contact died soon after Europeans arrived.

The main cause was disease, not war and not deliberate killing. Smallpox, measles, and other sicknesses had already spread through Europe, Asia, and Africa for hundreds of years. Many people there had built up some resistance. But these same sicknesses had never once reached the Americas before 1492. Native peoples had never been exposed to them. Their bodies had no built-in defense at all. Once a disease like smallpox arrived, it spread with terrifying speed. It traveled along the same paths people already used to deal with each other: trade routes, travel between villages, capture in raids, and forced labor. Often, a sickness reached a village faster than any European traveler did. It arrived like an invisible messenger, far ahead of any ship.

It matters, as a matter of accurate history, that this disaster was a result, not a plan. No European government sat down in 1493 and planned to wipe out a people using disease. In fact, no one in 1493 even understood how sickness spreads from person to person. The idea that tiny germs cause disease was not discovered for almost three hundred fifty more years. Calling this event a deliberate "genocide" suggests that someone planned it. The evidence does not support that. Using that word also hides the real cause: disease spreading through a population with no immunity, not any written-down policy. None of this makes the size of the loss any less real. None of it makes the loss any less worth taking seriously. The right question is not "what did someone want to happen?" The right question is this: what happens when two groups of people, with no shared history of disease, meet for the very first time? That answer had nothing to do with anyone's good or bad character. It had everything to do with biology neither side understood.

A Second Kind of Report, a Century Later

Columbus's letter is one example of a kind of writing you will see again in this course. Call it the sales-pitch exploration report. A writer creates this kind of document for one clear reason. An investor, a king, or the reading public back home needs to believe a far-off, risky voyage is still worth paying for. Almost a hundred years later, English writers produced their own version of this same kind of report. In 1585, a man named Thomas Hariot sailed to the new English colony at Roanoke. Roanoke sat on the coast of what is now North Carolina. Hariot went there to observe. He carefully wrote down everything he found, from plants and animals to the Algonquian peoples who already lived there. Three years later, in 1588, he published a book. He called it A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia. He wrote it partly to answer damaging rumors already spreading about the struggling colony. He also wanted to convince doubtful English readers that Roanoke was still worth backing.

Reading Hariot's report right after Columbus's letter lets you test something for yourself. Ninety-five years had passed between the two documents. A different European country was now paying for the voyage. The land, climate, and people looked very different too. Did any of those real differences change how Europeans wrote about the Americas? Or does the underlying purpose behind this kind of writing stay exactly the same, no matter which country, and no matter which century, is holding the pen?
