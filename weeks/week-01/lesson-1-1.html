📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
A few words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read; they'll also show up, underlined, in tomorrow's lesson.

circumference (ser-KUM-fer-ents) — the distance all the way around a circle, or all the way around a sphere like the Earth.

caravel (KARE-uh-vel) — a small, fast sailing ship built by the Portuguese that could sail closer into the wind than older ships.

astrolabe (AS-truh-layb) — a tool sailors used to find their ship's position by studying the stars.

Reconquista (ray-kon-KEE-stah) — the centuries-long effort by Christian kingdoms in Spain to take back land ruled by Muslim kingdoms, finished in January 1492.

📖 Today's Focus
Columbus did not prove the world was round. People already knew that. He won an argument about how big the world was, and he won it by being wrong in a lucky way.

In January 1492, the Spanish crown finished a seven-hundred-year project called the Reconquista: the conquest of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain. Nine months later, that same crown paid for a sailor's plan to sail west across open ocean until he reached Asia. These two facts did not cause each other directly. But without the first one, it is hard to picture the second one happening when it did. Watch for this pattern all year: one event, by chance, opens the door for another.

Why People Wanted a Western Sea Route

By 1492, educated people in Europe already knew the Earth was round. Scholars had known that since ancient times. The real argument was about something else: how big is the Earth? That answer decided how far a ship would have to sail west to reach Asia.

Europeans wanted a sea route to Asia for a clear reason: money. Silk and spices from Asia sold for high prices in Europe. For over a hundred years, traders had carried these goods overland through the eastern Mediterranean. Then in 1453, the Ottoman Empire captured the city of Constantinople, and that old route grew slower and far more expensive. Whoever found a new route by sea stood to make a fortune.

Portugal had been chasing that sea route for decades. Prince Henry of Portugal, later called "the Navigator," paid for ship after ship to explore down the coast of Africa. In 1488, the plan finally paid off: a Portuguese captain named Bartolomeu Dias sailed around the southern tip of Africa. He proved that a ship could reach the Indian Ocean, and from there, Asia. New tools made trips like this survivable. Sailors used the caravel, a small, fast ship; the magnetic compass, for finding direction; and the astrolabe, for finding position by the stars.

💡 Helpful to Know
Dias's 1488 voyage matters for a reason you might not guess: it gave Portugal's King John II a good reason to say no to Columbus. Portugal already had a working plan to reach Asia, by sailing around Africa. Why would the king risk money on an untested ocean route when a working route was already in hand? Columbus asked the one king most likely to help him, and got turned down for a perfectly reasonable reason.

An Argument Won by Being Wrong

Columbus's own plan rested on a real mistake. He used an old, small guess for the Earth's circumference, borrowed from an ancient geographer named Marinus of Tyre, instead of the larger, more accurate guess that most experts of his own day preferred. Using the small guess, Columbus decided Asia sat only about 2,400 miles west of the Canary Islands. The true distance is closer to 12,000 miles. Expert after expert, first in Portugal, then in Spain, reviewed his plan and turned it down. On the math, they were right to.

Here is the strange twist. If Columbus had guessed the distance correctly, no king or queen would have funded his trip at all, because everyone already knew 12,000 miles was too far for a ship of that time to sail on a few months of food and water. And if he had kept his wrong guess about the distance, but no land had happened to sit in between, his crew would have starved before ever reaching Asia. Columbus's plan worked only because two separate mistakes happened to cancel each other out: he underestimated the size of the Earth, and he had no way of knowing that an entire pair of continents sat in the ocean he meant to cross. No one in Europe could have known that second fact in 1492.

After years of being turned down again and again, Columbus finally found backers: Queen Isabella of Castile and King Ferdinand of Aragon agreed to fund his voyage in April 1492. Only months before, Spain had finished the Reconquista, so the royal treasury and the royal court finally had the money and the time to consider his request. Columbus's own persistence had put him in front of the right people. But it took Spain finishing a centuries-long war to make that moment arrive when it did.

The Crossing

Columbus's three ships, the Niña, the Pinta, and the Santa María, left the Spanish port of Palos on August 3, 1492. They stopped at the Canary Islands to load supplies and catch reliable winds blowing west. From there, they sailed into open ocean for five weeks: longer than any sailor on board had ever gone without seeing land. By early October, unease on the ships had grown close to mutiny. On October 12, a lookout aboard the Pinta spotted land. It was an island in the Bahamas; its Lucayan people called it Guanahaní. Columbus renamed it San Salvador.

Columbus never accepted that he had found something other than the outer edge of Asia. He called the people he met "Indians," a name that stuck for centuries. On his later voyages, he kept treating every new discovery as more proof of his own theory, even as his own crew began to doubt it. That stubbornness is worth noticing as a character trait, not just a mistake. The same refusal to hear "no" that carried his plan through a decade of rejection also blinded him to the plain evidence once he arrived. In the way this course uses the words virtue and vice, the same trait can point in two directions at once: it can carry you to success, or it can blind you to the truth, depending on the situation you are in.
