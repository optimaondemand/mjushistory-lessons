📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
A few words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read; they'll also show up, underlined, in tomorrow's lesson. Tap the speaker to hear each word.

cash crop (KASH KROP) — a crop grown mainly to be sold for money, often shipped far away, rather than eaten by the family that grew it.

indentured servant (in-DEN-cherd SUR-vunt) — a person who agreed to work without wages for a set number of years, often in exchange for passage across the Atlantic, and who was legally free at the end of that term.

subsistence (sub-SIS-tents) — farming or working to produce just enough for your own household to live on, with little or nothing left over to sell.

📖 Today's Focus
By 1700, an English colonist's daily life depended enormously on which of three regions he happened to live in.

All three regions flew the same flag and answered to the same king. They did not resemble each other much otherwise.

Start with New England. Its soil is rocky and its growing season is short, so it never supported large cash crop farming the way the Southern climate did. What New England built instead were small family farms raising enough to live on, which is called subsistence farming, and then real money came from somewhere else: fishing, shipbuilding, and trade.

New England also settled differently. Land there was granted to whole groups of settlers at once rather than to individuals, which produced tight, church-centered towns where neighbors lived within sight of each other. That is the village pattern you watched begin at Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay. Keep it in mind, because the South did nearly the opposite.

The Middle Colonies: A Different Mix Entirely

Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey, and Delaware had soil and weather well suited to wheat and other grains, grown at genuine commercial scale. The region earned the nickname "breadbasket" for shipping grain across the Atlantic world.

These colonies also became unusually mixed for English America, for two separate reasons. First, New York had not started out English at all. It was the Dutch colony of New Netherland until England seized it in 1664, and England inherited its Dutch, English, and other European settlers along with the territory. Second, Pennsylvania's founder William Penn deliberately recruited settlers of many faiths, including his own Quakers, alongside German and Scots-Irish immigrants.

Port cities grew up to move all that grain and trade. Philadelphia and New York became commercial hubs, which gave the Middle Colonies something neither neighbor had in the same measure: a serious farming economy and a serious urban one at the same time.

The South: Land, Labor, and Cash Crops

Virginia, Maryland, and the Carolinas built their economies on crops grown for export: tobacco in the Chesapeake, and further south, increasingly rice and indigo. These grew on large landholdings scattered across the countryside instead of gathered into towns.

Now put two facts side by side. Those crops demanded heavy, sustained labor, season after season. And the plantations were spread far apart, so no nearby town could supply a shared workforce the way a New England village could. Southern planters answered that labor problem first by importing indentured servants from England, and then, across the course of the 1600s, increasingly by enslaving Africans. That shift from one labor system to the other is one of the most consequential changes in American history, and you will examine it closely over the next two modules.

Maryland: A Refuge That Had to Be Written Down

Maryland deserves a note of its own. It was founded in 1634 by the Calvert family, who were English Catholics, as a place where Catholics could worship without the penalties they faced in Protestant England. That was the plan. What actually happened is that Protestant settlers arrived in large numbers and soon outnumbered the Catholics the colony had been built for.

So in 1649 Maryland's assembly passed the Act Concerning Religion, promising that no Christian in the colony would be troubled for how he worshipped. Read that carefully, because it cuts two ways. It is one of the earliest written guarantees of religious liberty in English America. It also protected only Christians, and it was passed by a Catholic minority that could see it was about to lose control. Liberty written down by people who need it is still liberty written down, and the writing outlasted the reason for it.

💡 Helpful to Know
Notice that no one sat down and chose these three economies. Each grew out of the particular soil, climate, and available labor that settlers actually found where they landed. This is the same reasoning you used in Module 3 to explain why Spain, France, England, and the Dutch pursued different strategies. The difference is the scale. There it explained differences between empires. Here it explains differences inside a single empire, among colonists who shared a language, a king, and a church.
