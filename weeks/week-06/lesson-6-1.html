📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
A few words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read; they'll also show up, underlined, in tomorrow's lesson. Tap the speaker to hear each word.

cash crop (KASH KROP) — a crop grown mainly to be sold for money, often shipped far away, rather than eaten by the family that grew it.

indentured servant (in-DEN-cherd SUR-vunt) — a person who agreed to work without wages for a set number of years, often in exchange for passage across the Atlantic, and who was legally free at the end of that term.

subsistence (sub-SIS-tents) — farming or working to produce just enough for your own household to live on, with little or nothing left over to sell.

📖 Today's Focus
By 1700, an English colonist's daily life depended enormously on which of three regions he happened to live in.

All three regions of English America flew the same flag. They answered to the same distant king back in London. They did not resemble each other much in any other important way.

Start with New England, the region you have already studied these past two weeks. Its soil is famously rocky and thin. It is poorly suited to large-scale farming. Its growing season is also short compared to regions further south. So New England never developed large cash crop farming the way the warmer South eventually did. What New England built instead were small family farms scattered across the countryside. These farms raised just barely enough food for a household to live on. This pattern is known as subsistence farming. Real money in New England came from somewhere else: fishing the coastal waters, building ships, and carrying goods in Atlantic trade.

New England also organized its land and settlements differently from other regions. Land there was typically granted all at once to whole groups of settlers, rather than parceled out to single families. This pattern produced tight, church-centered towns. Neighbors lived within easy sight of one another. They walked to the same meetinghouse each Sunday. That is the same village pattern you already watched begin at Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay. Keep this pattern in mind, because the Southern colonies developed in nearly the opposite direction.

The Middle Colonies: A Different Mix Entirely

Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey, and Delaware together made up what historians call the Middle Colonies. This region had soil and weather well suited to growing wheat and other grains, at a real commercial scale. The whole region earned the nickname "breadbasket" for shipping grain across the Atlantic trading world.

These colonies became unusually mixed for English America, for two separate reasons. First, New York had not started out as an English colony at all. It was the Dutch colony of New Netherland, until England seized the territory in 1664. England simply inherited its Dutch, English, and other European settlers along with the conquered territory. Second, Pennsylvania's founder, William Penn, deliberately recruited settlers of many faiths. He recruited his own Quaker community. He also recruited German and Scots-Irish immigrants, seeking land and religious tolerance.

Busy port cities grew up to move all that grain and trade across the Atlantic. Philadelphia and New York both became major commercial hubs, bustling with merchants, ships, and warehouses. That combination gave the Middle Colonies something neither neighbor had in the same measure. It had a serious commercial farming economy and a serious urban economy, side by side.

The South: Land, Labor, and Cash Crops

Virginia, Maryland, and the Carolinas built their economies around crops grown for export: tobacco throughout the Chesapeake, and further south, rice and indigo. These cash crops grew on large landholdings scattered across the countryside. They were not gathered tightly into towns the way New England farming was.

Now put two facts side by side. Those export crops demanded heavy, sustained labor, season after season, with no real break. Southern plantations were also spread far apart from one another. No nearby town could supply a shared workforce, the way a compact New England village could. Southern planters answered this labor problem in two stages. First, throughout much of the 1600s, they imported large numbers of indentured servants from England. Then, across the rest of that century, they increasingly turned to enslaving Africans permanently instead. That shift from one labor system to the other stands as one of the most consequential changes in American history. You will examine this shift closely over the next two weeks.

Maryland: A Refuge That Had to Be Written Down

Maryland deserves a note of its own in this week's survey. It was founded in 1634 by the powerful Calvert family. The Calverts were English Catholics, a religious minority facing real legal penalties back in Protestant England. They wanted a colony where fellow Catholics could worship freely, without those same penalties. That was the Calverts' original plan, at least. What actually happened turned out differently. Protestant settlers soon arrived in large, growing numbers. The same land and opportunity that attracted Catholics drew them too. They eventually outnumbered the very Catholic population the colony had been built to protect.

So in 1649, facing this changed reality, Maryland's assembly passed the Act Concerning Religion. It promised that no Christian in the colony would be troubled for how he worshipped. Read that promise carefully, though, because it cuts two different ways. It stands today as one of the earliest written guarantees of religious liberty in English America. It also protected only Christians, leaving out anyone of other faiths. And a Catholic minority passed it, one that could see it was about to lose political control of the colony it had founded. Liberty written down by people who urgently need it for themselves is still genuine liberty. The written law outlasted the political reason that first produced it.

💡 Helpful to Know
Notice that no one sat down and chose these three economies. Each grew out of the particular soil, climate, and available labor that settlers actually found where they landed. This is the same reasoning you used in Module 3 to explain why Spain, France, England, and the Dutch pursued different strategies. The difference is the scale. There it explained differences between empires. Here it explains differences inside a single empire. These colonists shared a language, a king, and a church.
