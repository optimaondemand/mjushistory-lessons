📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
A few words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read; they will also show up, underlined, in tomorrow's lesson. Tap the speaker to hear each word.

chattel slavery (CHAT-ul SLAY-vur-ee) — a system of slavery that treats an enslaved person as property, able to be bought, sold, moved, and passed down to that person's children, with no end date and no legal rights of their own.

corsair (KOR-sair) — a pirate, especially one of the raiders from North Africa's Barbary Coast who attacked ships and coastal towns and carried off captives to sell as slaves.

serfdom (SURF-dum) — a system, common in medieval Europe, in which peasants called serfs owed labor to a lord and could not leave his land, but were not bought and sold as individual property the way enslaved people were.

📖 Today's Focus
To understand what was new about the Atlantic slave trade, you first have to understand what was not new: slavery itself.

Slavery is one of the oldest human institutions. People practiced it across Asia, Europe, and Africa for thousands of years before Columbus ever set sail. Long before any European ship reached the coast of West Africa, long-distance trade routes already carried enslaved people alongside gold, salt, ivory, and cloth. Trans-Saharan caravans crossed the desert. Indian Ocean sailors moved along Africa's eastern coast. Both routes had carried captives for centuries.

Arab and Ottoman traders ran large slave-trading networks of their own, with no connection to the Americas at all. So did the corsairs of North Africa's Barbary Coast. These were pirates who raided ships and towns, including towns on the coast of Europe itself. Between the 1500s and the 1700s, Barbary corsairs carried off thousands of Europeans as captives. Slavery, in other words, was not a European invention, and Europeans were not only its practitioners. At times, they were its victims too.

Slavery and Serfdom: Related, Not Identical

You may already know a little about serfdom from earlier history study. It is worth comparing to slavery here, because the two are often confused with each other.

Medieval European serfs owed labor to a lord and could not freely leave his land. In that narrow sense, serfdom restricted a person's freedom the way slavery did. But serfs were not bought and sold as individual property, separate from the land they worked. They usually kept certain customary rights, and their unfree status, while inherited, was tied to a place, not to a person the way property is. A serf was not, under the law, the same kind of thing as an ox or a cart.

Many African societies practiced their own forms of slavery long before Europeans arrived, and these often worked differently still. In some African societies, a person's slave status could be temporary. It might be tied to a debt or to capture in war, and it did not always pass automatically and permanently to that person's children.

Chattel slavery is different from both of these systems. It is the term historians use for slavery that treats a person as property, plain and simple: property that can be bought, sold, moved, and inherited independent of any land, and passed down to children with no end date and no path out. That is not what serfdom was. And, as you just read, it was not always what slavery meant in Africa before European contact, either. Chattel slavery is specifically the system that would harden into law in the English colonies, and it is the system this module's lessons trace from its beginning.

💡 Helpful to Know
This comparison matters for a specific reason. It lets you ask a sharper historical question than simply "did slavery exist before Europeans arrived?" The answer to that one is yes, widely, and comparing it to serfdom and older African practices shows why. The sharper question is this: what specifically changed when European traders entered existing African trade networks, and at what scale? That is the question this module's lessons are built around.

Where European Traders Entered an Existing System

When Portuguese ships first reached the coast of West Africa in the 1400s, they did not invent slave trading there. They made contact with African kingdoms that already practiced it, kingdoms like Benin, Dahomey, and the Kongo. These kingdoms were willing to trade captives to Europeans in exchange for goods, especially firearms and cloth, for their own reasons of profit and political advantage over their rivals. Spain, and later England, France, and the Dutch Republic, all followed the Portuguese into this same trade over the next two centuries, each competing for access to the same African ports and the same African trading partners.

European traders rarely captured people themselves in large numbers. Instead, they overwhelmingly purchased captives who had already been taken in African wars, raids, or judicial punishment. What Europeans added was something different: they organized and financed the transport of captives across the Atlantic Ocean, at a scale and a permanence that no earlier trade network had ever approached.

That distinction is essential to getting this history right. Contact with an existing system is not the same thing as inventing a new one. Getting the distinction right does not make what followed any less serious. If anything, it makes the history more accurate, and a more accurate history is what the rest of this module asks of you.
