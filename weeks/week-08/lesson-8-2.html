Lesson 8.2
The Great Awakening Shakes New England
Lesson · ~810 words · ~7 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
A few words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read.

denomination (dih-nom-uh-NAY-shun) — a distinct branch or organized group within a religion, such as Baptists or Presbyterians within Protestant Christianity.

itinerant (eye-TIN-ur-unt) — traveling from place to place rather than staying settled in one location, especially a preacher who moved from town to town instead of leading a single congregation.

revivalist (rih-VY-vul-ist) — a preacher or leader whose sermons are meant to renew and intensify religious feeling and commitment among people who may already consider themselves believers.

📖 Today's Focus
By the 1730s, many New England ministers worried their congregations had grown comfortable, routine, and only outwardly religious.

A full century after Massachusetts Bay's founding, some worried ministers and outside observers grew concerned about their own religious community. They believed the founding generation's intense religious commitment had cooled into mere formal habit, with little genuine inward conviction behind it. This is exactly the concern Edwards's sermon addresses, the sermon you just finished reading. Beginning in the 1730s and peaking in the early 1740s, a powerful wave of revivalist preaching swept through the colonies. Historians call it the Great Awakening. It drew enormous crowds wherever it went. It provoked intense emotional responses in ordinary listeners. It ultimately divided colonial religious life into rival camps. Historians estimate that a large share of all colonists alive at the time heard at least one revivalist sermon in person, during the Awakening's peak years. That reach is extraordinary for any single religious movement in this era. Remember, this was an age with no fast printing presses, and no good roads, to make that reach easy. Whitefield's own printed sermons circulated widely too, spreading his message even to towns he never personally visited.

The Itinerant Who Filled Fields

George Whitefield was an English Anglican minister by training. He became the Great Awakening's single most famous figure. Beginning in 1739, he traveled tirelessly through the American colonies as an itinerant preacher. He never settled permanently in any one congregation. His dramatic open-air sermons drew enormous crowds, sometimes in the many thousands. That was more people than actually lived in many entire colonial towns. His preaching style was theatrical and emotionally powerful, honed through constant practice on the road. Trained ministers and ordinary colonists alike found his skill remarkable, unlike anything they had witnessed before. Whitefield preached openly to any colonist willing to listen, regardless of denomination, hometown, or colony. That inclusive approach made him one of the first genuinely trans-colonial celebrities in American history. He gave colonists scattered across many separate colonies a rare shared experience, decades before any political movement would attempt the same thing. Some ministers worried that Whitefield's celebrity mattered more to audiences than the actual substance of what he preached.

Whitefield was not the only prominent revivalist voice active during this period, though he remained the most famous. Gilbert Tennent, a Presbyterian minister in New Jersey, preached and published a provocative sermon in 1740, titled The Danger of an Unconverted Ministry. In it, he argued that some settled, formally trained clergy lacked authentic conversion experiences themselves, despite their education and ordained position. He bluntly said such ministers were unfit to lead their own congregations. This was a serious, deliberately public accusation, aimed directly at fellow ordained ministers. It shows that the Awakening's central argument was not only about how ordinary believers should feel inwardly. It was also about who had the legitimate standing to lead a congregation at all.

Tennent's own Presbyterian denomination split sharply over this question, splintering into two rival camps almost immediately. The "New Side" faction actively supported the ongoing revival and its methods. It even ran its own training school, the Log College, to produce a steady stream of revival-friendly ministers. The "Old Side" faction sided instead with settled congregational order and established clerical authority. It viewed Tennent's accusation as a dangerous attack on legitimate church authority. The two rival factions did not formally reunite for well over fifteen years. That long estrangement affected ordinary church members too, not just ministers, since families sometimes found themselves worshipping in different congregations over the very same dispute. If a single denomination could split this deeply over the Great Awakening, imagine how much wider the disagreement ran across all of colonial Protestantism.

Old Lights and New Lights

The Great Awakening ultimately split colonial Protestantism into two large, opposing camps. "New Lights" enthusiastically embraced the revival's emotional intensity and its itinerant preaching. They saw both as genuine signs of authentic divine work among ordinary people. This position followed directly from Edwards's own distinction between mere notional belief and true spiritual "sense," which you read about yesterday. "Old Lights" included many established, formally trained clergy who remained skeptical of the whole movement. They viewed the revival's emotional excesses with suspicion: public fainting, loud crying out, and believers breaking with settled congregational authority to follow traveling preachers. They worried that raw emotional intensity was simply being mistaken for lasting faith. Both sides accused the other of betraying the true spirit of the founding generation, each certain that it alone carried that inheritance forward. This deep division produced lasting splits within many existing churches. It led to entirely new congregations founded across the colonies. Eventually, it produced whole new denominations and institutions too, including several new colleges founded specifically by New Light Protestants. These Protestants had grown dissatisfied with existing colleges dominated by Old Light thinking.

💡 Helpful to Know
Not every observer of Whitefield shared his theology. Benjamin Franklin was a printer and civic leader in Philadelphia, with no strong denominational commitment of his own. He attended Whitefield's sermons repeatedly. He published Whitefield's writings for profit. And he admired Whitefield's organizational skill and charitable fundraising. At the same time, Franklin remained unconvinced by, and sometimes gently amused by, Whitefield's theology itself. You will read Franklin's account next. It gives you a rare thing in this course: a skeptical, largely secular eyewitness account of a deeply religious movement.
