📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary (two words continued from Lesson 17.2)

Anti-Federalist (AN-ty-FED-er-uh-list) — an opponent of the Constitution who feared it gave the federal government too much power.

bicameral (by-KAM-er-ul) — having two separate lawmaking chambers, or houses, such as the House of Representatives and the Senate.

delegate (DEL-uh-git) — a person chosen to represent and act on behalf of a larger group at a meeting.

Federalist (FED-er-uh-list) — a supporter of the new Constitution who wanted a stronger national government.

📖 Today's Focus
The Constitution needed nine of thirteen state conventions to say yes. Several of the largest and most important states nearly said no instead.

Ratification required approval from conventions in nine of the thirteen states. The debate split Americans into two camps. Federalists included James Madison, Alexander Hamilton, and John Jay. They defended the new Constitution's stronger national government. They published dozens of essays, collected as The Federalist, in New York newspapers. Their goal was to persuade skeptical voters. Madison, Hamilton, and Jay wrote all 85 of these essays under a single shared pseudonym: Publius. That name honored a legendary founder of the Roman Republic. Readers at the time did not know which of the three men had written any specific essay. The shared name let the argument stand on its own. Readers could not weigh it based on whose reputation stood behind it instead. Anti-Federalists included Patrick Henry and George Mason. They argued the new government concentrated dangerous, unchecked power. That power reminded them of the very monarchy the Revolution had fought to escape. They objected specifically that the Constitution contained no bill of rights. Such a bill would have explicitly protected individual liberties from federal overreach. Many state constitutions already in force had one. The new federal Constitution did not.

Anti-Federalists wrote under pseudonyms too, echoing the Federalists' own approach. A New York writer using the name "Brutus," another figure from Roman history, published an influential series of essays opposing ratification. Historians still debate his true identity to this very day, though many suspect the New York judge Robert Yates. Both sides of this debate, in other words, reached for the same classical Roman names to lend their arguments weight, authority, and a sense of timeless, principled purpose.

A Compromise That Secured Ratification

Ratification remained genuinely uncertain in several pivotal states. Massachusetts, Virginia, and New York were among them. Virginia's ratifying convention approved the Constitution by a margin of only ten votes, out of over 160 cast. New York's convention approved it by an even narrower margin: three votes. That approval came only after word arrived that enough other states had already ratified. The Constitution's adoption was now a near certainty, regardless of New York's own decision. These were not comfortable victories. A relatively small number of delegates, in either state, could have changed the outcome entirely, and everyone in that room knew it.

Delaware, New Jersey, and Georgia ratified quickly and without much controversy, early in the process. Pennsylvania's convention became the first real test after that. Anti-Federalist delegates in the Pennsylvania legislature tried to prevent a quorum by simply staying away. They hoped to delay the ratifying convention itself before it could even begin. A Philadelphia crowd forcibly dragged two of these missing delegates back to the State House to force a vote. Pennsylvania ratified soon afterward anyway, once a quorum was finally forced. But the episode showed how contested even the process of convening a convention could become, not just the final vote itself.

Massachusetts held its own convention early, in February 1788, before Virginia or New York had acted. Its vote was closer than many expected too: 187 to 168 in favor. Massachusetts delegates first proposed the idea of pairing ratification with a formal recommendation for a bill of rights. Other pivotal states borrowed this exact strategy afterward. A solution invented under pressure in one state convention became the template that saved ratification in several others.

The decisive compromise that secured their approval was a promise. Federalist leaders in these states pledged to support a series of amendments protecting individual rights. This would happen once the new government convened. It addressed the Anti-Federalists' central objection. It did not require an entirely new convention to do so. Congress proposed twelve such amendments in 1789. Ten were ratified by the states in 1791, becoming the Bill of Rights. The debate did not simply end when the Constitution took effect. It produced a durable, additional layer of the founding document. That layer exists specifically because Anti-Federalist objections were taken seriously, rather than simply outvoted.

Alexander Hamilton made an unusual argument of his own in Federalist No. 84. He argued a bill of rights was not just unnecessary, but potentially dangerous. Listing specific protected rights, he warned, might imply the government held power over anything not explicitly listed. Madison and other Federalists eventually judged this argument less persuasive than the practical need to secure ratification. They set Hamilton's specific worry aside once the political bargain became clear.

Notice what this pattern means for how you should read the whole ratification story. The Constitution's opponents lost the vote in every state that mattered. But they did not lose the argument entirely. Their central concern became permanent, written law within just a few years of their own defeat at the ballot box.

💡 Helpful to Know
The Bill of Rights, in the form we know it, exists for a specific reason. The Constitution's opponents raised an objection serious enough that its supporters had to accommodate it to win. This is worth remembering whenever a historical outcome looks, in hindsight, like the obviously "correct" or inevitable one. The Bill of Rights was a contingent political compromise. It was not a feature the Constitution's authors originally planned to include.
