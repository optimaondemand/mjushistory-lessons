Parliament's Response: The Intolerable Acts

📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Two new words below, plus two carried over from yesterday.

counterfactual (kown-ter-FAK-choo-ul) — a “what if” question that imagines how history might have gone differently if one specific event had happened another way.

monopoly (muh-NAH-puh-lee) — complete control over the sale of a good or service by one seller, with no real competition.

punitive (PYOO-nuh-tiv) — meant to punish.

tacitly (TASS-it-lee) — without saying so directly; by silent agreement or implication.

📖 Today's Focus
Parliament decided to make an example of Massachusetts. The other colonies decided Massachusetts's punishment was their own problem too, and acted accordingly.

Parliament responded to the Tea Party in 1774 with a set of punitive laws. Colonists soon nicknamed them the "Intolerable Acts." Parliament itself called them the Coercive Acts. The Boston Port Act closed Boston's harbor to trade entirely. It stayed closed until the destroyed tea was paid for. This devastated the city's economy. The Massachusetts Government Act stripped much of the colony's traditional self-government. It converted the elected council into a crown-appointed body instead. It sharply restricted town meetings. That struck directly at the same New England town-meeting tradition you studied in Module 10. The Administration of Justice Act allowed a different kind of change. Royal officials accused of crimes committed in the course of duty could now be tried outside Massachusetts. This put them beyond the reach of local juries, widely seen as biased against them. The Quartering Act authorized housing British troops in private buildings, not just existing barracks.

The Massachusetts Government Act struck a nerve far beyond Massachusetts itself. Nearly every colony operated under some kind of charter, as you learned in Module 10. If Parliament could simply rewrite Massachusetts's charter by ordinary legislation, any colony's charter could be rewritten the same way. No colony's own consent would be needed. This is exactly why so many colonies treated Massachusetts's crisis as their own. It was not abstract sympathy. It was a direct, practical threat to every colonial assembly's own foundation, and every assembly in every colony understood exactly what was at stake for itself.

Colonists lumped a fifth law in with these four. Parliament had not actually designed this fifth law as punishment at all. The Quebec Act, passed the same year, extended the province of Quebec's boundaries deep into the Ohio Country. It also guaranteed French Catholic settlers there the right to practice their own religion and legal customs. Many Protestant colonists, especially in New England, read this as yet another sign of an untrustworthy Parliament. Their reasoning went like this. Parliament was willing to expand a Catholic-tolerating colony's boundaries. It expanded them into land colonists themselves had fought a war to secure. A government willing to do that, the reasoning went, could not be trusted with their own rights either. The suspicion was not really about Quebec at all. It was about what Parliament might do next, closer to home.

The Backfire

Consider a counterfactual directly. Suppose Parliament had imposed these punitive measures on Massachusetts alone, and nothing more happened. Parliament expected the other twelve colonies to stay quiet. It hoped Massachusetts would absorb the punishment while the rest of the colonies were spared. That is roughly what Parliament actually hoped would happen. It is not what happened. Virginia's House of Burgesses, the very assembly you studied in Module 6, declared a day of fasting and prayer in solidarity with Boston. Virginia's royal governor dissolved the Burgesses in response. Its members simply reconvened at a nearby tavern to keep organizing anyway. Other colonies recognized something important. A Parliament willing to gut one colony's charter and self-government could do the same to any of them next. So they sent food, supplies, and formal declarations of solidarity to Massachusetts. They did this at real cost and risk to themselves. Fellow-feeling among colonists had been cultivated over decades of shared grievance, as you've traced across this whole quarter. By 1774, that fellow-feeling proved strong enough. Isolating any single colony was a strategy that could no longer work.

None of this was guaranteed in advance. A colony as different from Massachusetts as South Carolina had no obvious reason to help. South Carolina had a rice-and-indigo plantation economy. It had its own distinct history, far from New England. Yet it still risked royal anger over a dispute that began in a Boston harbor, hundreds of miles away. Colonists made a specific choice. They chose to treat an attack on one colony's charter as an attack on every colony's charter. That choice, not any natural or automatic unity among thirteen separate colonies, is what the First Continental Congress represented.

Georgia was the one colony that sent no delegates to the First Continental Congress at all. Georgia depended heavily on British military help against nearby Native nations and Spanish Florida. Its royal governor also kept tighter control over local politics than most other colonies allowed. Georgia's absence is a useful reminder. Colonial solidarity in 1774 was real and strong, but it was not universal or automatic. Each colony weighed its own particular circumstances before choosing to join the rest. Solidarity, in other words, was built one deliberate choice at a time, not simply assumed. It had to be chosen again and again by each colony, and it could have failed at any point along the way.

💡 Helpful to Know
This response drove the calling of the First Continental Congress in Philadelphia, in September 1774. It brought delegates from twelve colonies together to coordinate a unified response. That response included a colony-wide boycott agreement, the Continental Association. You'll trace this Congress's escalating actions in the weeks ahead, right through to Lexington and Concord and the Declaration of Independence.
