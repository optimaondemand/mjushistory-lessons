📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
A few words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read; they'll also show up, underlined, in tomorrow's lesson.

charter (CHAR-ter) — a legal document from a king or government that gives a person or group the right to settle, trade, or govern a piece of land.

joint-stock company (joynt-stok KUM-puh-nee) — a business owned by many investors, who buy shares and share in its profits and losses.

proprietary (proh-PRY-uh-tair-ee) — owned and controlled by one person, called a proprietor, who holds special legal rights over it.

selectmen (suh-LEKT-men) — town officials, elected at a town meeting, who handle the town's everyday business.

📖 Today's Focus
By 1750, an ordinary New England farmer had more regular, direct experience governing his own community than most people anywhere else in the world.

Welcome to Quarter 2. Think back on everything you studied last quarter. You met the House of Burgesses in Module 6. You met the Mayflower Compact in Module 5. You met Massachusetts Bay's covenant theology in Module 8. Each of these already showed English colonists building habits of self-government. They built these habits piece by piece. Most colonists were not following some grand plan. They were solving one practical problem at a time. This quarter picks up that same thread. It follows the thread all the way to the American Revolution.

Charters as Foundations

Every English colony operated under some kind of charter. A charter was a legal document. A king or a government used it to grant someone the right to settle, trade, or govern land. There were three main kinds. A royal charter came directly from the crown. A joint-stock company charter was owned by many investors. Virginia's original charter worked this way. A proprietary charter gave one favored person broad authority over a whole territory. William Penn held Pennsylvania this way.

Charters varied enormously in how much self-government they allowed. Still, nearly every English colony ended up with an elected assembly of some kind. This assembly gave property-holding male colonists a voice in local law. But there was a catch. These colonial governments still answered to a distant crown. The king could grant a charter. The king could also take it away. Massachusetts Bay learned this the hard way later in the century. Its original charter was revoked. A new one arrived with tighter royal control. Self-government under a charter was real, but it depended on the crown's continued goodwill. It was not a right the colonists held on their own.

Virginia's Head Start: The House of Burgesses

You already met one of these assemblies in Module 6. In 1619, Virginia's colonists organized the House of Burgesses. It was the first elected legislative body anywhere in English America. Burgesses were representatives. Property-holding men elected them. Burgesses met to pass local laws and set local policy. The Virginia Company, back in London, still held final authority over the colony. But the Company had made a practical choice. Distant colonists needed some voice in decisions that affected their daily lives. That single practical choice in 1619 set a pattern. Nearly every English colony founded afterward eventually copied it in some form. An elected assembly became the normal expectation, not the exception. By the time Massachusetts Bay and Connecticut built their own systems, an elected voice in government was already becoming an American habit, not a novelty.

The Town Meeting: Democracy at the Smallest Scale

New England's town meeting system gave ordinary colonists their most direct experience of self-government. Each town held an annual meeting. Many towns also held extra special meetings when needed. At these meetings, property-holding male residents gathered together. They elected local officials called selectmen. They set the town's tax rates. They decided on roads and other public works. They settled other town business by direct vote. This kind of participation had no match at the colony level. As you saw in Module 8, colony-wide elections in Massachusetts Bay often restricted voting to full church members. That was a narrow group. A man who could never vote for the colony's governor might still speak up at his own town meeting. He might propose an idea there. He might vote directly on his town's budget every single year.

Connecticut Writes Its Own Rules

Massachusetts Bay was not the only New England colony experimenting with written self-government. In 1639, settlers in the Connecticut River Valley adopted a plan called the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut. These settlers wrote this plan for themselves. No distant king or company handed it to them. The plan explained how each town would choose its representatives. It explained how much power the governor would hold. It explained how much power the assembly would hold. Some historians call it one of the first written constitutions in the English-speaking world. The exact label matters less than the plain fact underneath it. Ordinary settlers were now writing down their own rules, in advance, for their own government to follow.

Pennsylvania took a very different path to a similar place. William Penn held his colony as a personal grant from the king. That start was about as far from the colonists' own initiative as a charter could get. Yet in 1682, Penn made a surprising choice. He voluntarily wrote and adopted his own Frame of Government. This frame guaranteed an elected assembly. It also guaranteed broad religious liberty to any colonist who settled there. A proprietor held total legal authority over Pennsylvania. He chose to share that authority anyway. This is worth remembering. Self-government in the colonies did not always have to be won through force or protest. Sometimes, it was simply offered.

💡 Helpful to Know
Historian Alexis de Tocqueville wrote about American democracy nearly two centuries later. He specifically credited New England's town-meeting tradition with teaching ordinary Americans the practical habits of self-government, from the ground up. This happened long before any national constitution existed to enshrine those habits in law. Practice preceded theory here, not the other way around.
