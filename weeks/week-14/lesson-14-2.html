📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
A few words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read.

natural rights (NACH-uh-rul rites) — rights every person has simply by being human, which no government gave and no government can fairly take away.

consent (kun-SENT) — agreement or permission given freely.

unalienable (un-AYL-yuh-nuh-bul) — impossible to take away or give away, even by the person who has it.

despotism (DESS-puh-tiz-um) — a form of government in which one ruler holds total, unchecked power.

📖 Today's Focus
John Adams later remembered arguing that Thomas Jefferson, not himself, should write the document that would define the new nation's founding ideas, and he gave two specific, practical reasons why.

In June 1776, Richard Henry Lee proposed a resolution to Congress. It formally called for the colonies to declare independence. Congress appointed a Committee of Five to draft a formal declaration explaining that resolution. The five members were Thomas Jefferson, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Robert Livingston, and Roger Sherman. According to Adams's later account, he convinced the committee that Jefferson, not himself, should write the first draft. Adams reasoned that Jefferson was the better writer. He also thought his own reputation was too "obnoxious," Adams's own word for it. He believed himself too politically divisive for the job. Leading the writing of a document meant to unite colonial opinion needed someone less polarizing than John Adams. It is a small, telling moment: a man famous for his own strong ego chose, on this one occasion, to step aside.

Congress did not adopt Jefferson's draft unchanged. Delegates debated and edited it for two full days. They cut roughly a quarter of his original text. The most significant cut was a passage blaming King George III for the transatlantic slave trade itself. Jefferson's draft held the king responsible both for introducing slavery to the colonies and for exciting enslaved people to rise up against their enslavers. Delegates from Georgia and South Carolina objected strongly. Some New England delegates objected too, since their own merchants profited from the slave trade. Congress struck the passage entirely. Think carefully about who wrote that passage, and about who chose to cut it out. The finished Declaration's silence on slavery is itself part of this document's real history, a fact worth sitting with rather than skipping past.

A Different Kind of Argument

The Declaration's structure does specific, careful work. It opens with a general theory of why government exists and when its power is fair. This theory comes directly from John Locke's Second Treatise of Government. Governments exist to protect people's natural rights. A government's power is only fair if it comes from the consent of the people it governs. Suppose a government works against those rights instead of protecting them. Then the people have the right to give up their loyalty to it. After laying out this theory, the document shifts to something different. It moves to a long, specific list of complaints. Every one of them is aimed personally at King George III.

This continues a shift Common Sense had already started. That shift moved blame away from Parliament. It moved blame onto the King himself, personally. Aiming at one person, instead of "the British government" in the abstract, served the document's argument well. A government that had lost its fair claim to rule needed one clear, identifiable party who had lost it. Naming a single person made the argument sharper. It also made the argument easier for ordinary readers to follow, the same lesson Paine had already taught with Common Sense's success.

Notice how the document's two halves depend on each other. The theory section alone would be abstract philosophy, with no specific complaint attached to it. The grievance list alone would be a mere complaint, without any larger justification behind it. Together, the two halves make a complete argument. They show both why revolution can ever be justified in principle, and why this specific revolution was justified in this specific case.

The Grievances Echo the Whole Quarter

The Declaration's specific list of grievances is not new material. Nearly every complaint on it echoes something you've already studied this quarter. "He has kept among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the Consent of our legislatures" recalls the troops stationed in Boston in Module 11. "He has dissolved Representative Houses repeatedly" recalls Virginia's House of Burgesses being dissolved in Module 12. "For imposing Taxes on us without our Consent" recalls the entire argument over the Stamp Act and Townshend Acts. The Declaration was not inventing new complaints in 1776. It was gathering nearly a decade of specific, already-lived grievances into one final, formal list.

Once Congress approved the final text, it needed to reach ordinary people quickly. Printer John Dunlap worked through the night of July 4 to produce copies known as the Dunlap broadside. These copies were sent out by horseback rider to colonial assemblies, army officers, and newspapers. Town criers and officials read the Declaration aloud in public squares up and down the colonies within days. For most colonists, hearing the document read aloud was how they first encountered its words, not reading a printed copy themselves. Imagine standing in a town square that week. A stranger reads these words aloud for the first time. No one listening yet knows how the war will turn out.

💡 Helpful to Know
Congress voted for independence on July 2, 1776. It formally adopted the Declaration's final text on July 4, the date now celebrated. Most delegates, though, did not actually sign the finished copy until August. When Congress voted on July 2, twelve colonies voted in favor. New York's delegation abstained; its instructions from home had not yet been updated to permit a yes vote, though New York's own legislature endorsed independence within weeks. John Adams believed, at the time, that July 2 would be remembered as America's true anniversary. He turned out to be wrong about the date, but not about the underlying judgment: a vote genuinely had been taken, and the document explaining it came only after that vote, not before it. The document's purpose was not to create independence; Congress's vote had already done that. Its purpose was to justify independence, to Americans, to the world, and to history.
