Common Sense Changes the Conversation

📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary (continued from Lesson 13.2)

Loyalist (LOY-uh-list) — a colonist who remained loyal to the king and opposed independence from Britain.

reconciliation (rek-un-sil-ee-AY-shun) — making up after a disagreement and restoring a peaceful relationship.

📖 Today's Focus
A 47-page pamphlet, written in plain language for ordinary readers, sold roughly 150,000 copies in a few months. It helped push a still-divided public toward a conclusion most colonists were not ready to accept just a year earlier.

Thomas Paine published Common Sense without his name on it. This happened in January 1776. Right around that same time, news of the Olive Branch Petition's rejection was spreading. Dickinson's Letters from a Pennsylvania Farmer (Module 11) and the Olive Branch Petition (Module 13) had both argued carefully. Both had still stayed loyal to the crown. Paine attacked that whole approach head-on. He did not just argue against this king's specific policies. He argued against the whole idea of hereditary monarchy itself. Hereditary monarchy meant a king whose power passes down through his own family. Paine argued against that entire system of government. He made the case for full, immediate independence. He wrote in language any literate colonist could follow and repeat, not just a trained lawyer.

An Unlikely Author

Thomas Paine had lived in America for barely a year when he wrote Common Sense. He was born in England. He had failed at several trades there, including making corsets and collecting taxes. Then he met Benjamin Franklin in London. Franklin encouraged him to emigrate. Franklin gave him letters of introduction to Philadelphia. Paine arrived in America in late 1774. He had no fortune. He had no established reputation. He had no formal political standing of any kind. Within about a year, he had written the single most influential pamphlet of the entire Revolutionary period. Few figures in this course illustrate contingency more sharply than Paine does. A struggling Englishman, recently arrived, reshaped how an entire continent argued about its own future.

Paine made one argument in particular that stuck with readers. It made no sense, he wrote, for a small island three thousand miles away to permanently govern a growing continent. Geography itself, not just politics, argued for separation, in Paine's view. A relationship that might have made some sense when the colonies were small and dependent looked increasingly absurd now. America's population and resources were growing far faster than Britain's own.

Why Plain Language Mattered

Paine chose to write in simple, everyday prose. This was different from the formal style favored by educated writers like Dickinson. This was not just a matter of taste. It was a real political strategy. A document meant to move broad public opinion needed a different approach. It needed something other than a style aimed at a handful of lawmakers or a distant king. It needed to reach readers who had never studied formal rhetoric or English law. Common Sense's huge sales numbers suggest the strategy worked. Compare those numbers to the colonies' small total population at the time. The pamphlet became one of the most widely read documents in the colonies. It reached readers across every social class. Few earlier pamphlets had managed that. It also reached readers well beyond the thirteen colonies. The pamphlet was quickly reprinted in Britain. It was translated into French too. It circulated in Europe within months of its American publication. Few pamphlets of any era have traveled that fast or that far. Paine had written for ordinary American readers specifically. But his argument about the illegitimacy of hereditary monarchy found an audience wherever monarchy itself was being questioned.

George Washington himself noticed the pamphlet's power. He later wrote that Common Sense was "working a wonderful change" in the minds of many men. He had copies read aloud to his own troops outside Boston. A general already leading an army now found the political argument for independence catching up with the war he was already fighting.

Paine did not invent every argument colonists were already making. Many colonists had already lost patience with the crown after Bunker Hill, and after the King's rejection of the Olive Branch Petition, as you read last week. What Paine did was gather these separate frustrations into one sustained, readable argument. He gave colonists a shared vocabulary for saying out loud what many were already starting to think privately.

Agreeing on Independence, Disagreeing on What Comes Next

John Adams's own reaction to Common Sense was more complicated than simple admiration. He agreed completely with Paine's case for independence. He thought the pamphlet had done more to shift public opinion than anything else written that year. But Adams strongly disliked one specific part of Paine's proposal. Paine wanted a single-house legislature with no separate executive to check it. Adams thought that design invited exactly the kind of unchecked power the colonies were trying to escape in the first place. He wrote his own pamphlet later in 1776, called Thoughts on Government. He wrote it specifically to counter Paine's constitutional ideas. He still endorsed Paine's call for independence, though. Agreeing on the destination did not mean agreeing on the design of what came next.

💡 Helpful to Know
Think about the contingency here. If Paine had written in the same dense, legal style as Dickinson, would Common Sense have reached the same wide audience, or caused the same fast shift in public opinion? A great idea, delivered in the wrong style, can fail to move anyone at all. In this pamphlet, form and content worked together to create an outsized impact on history.
