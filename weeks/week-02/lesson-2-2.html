Lesson 2.2
Spain's American Empire Takes Shape
Lesson · ~1,000 words · ~8 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
One new word below, plus one carried over from yesterday. Look them over before you read.

tribute (TRIB-yoot) — payments of goods, food, or wealth that a weaker or conquered group must regularly give to a more powerful ruler.

viceroy (VYSE-roy) — a person appointed by a king to govern a colony or territory in the king's name.

📖 Today's Focus
Conquest was the fast part. Governing what had been conquered took the rest of the century, and Spain never fully solved the problem.

After Tenochtitlan fell in 1521, Spain faced a difficult practical question. The question had nothing to do with fighting. Every conquering power eventually faces this same question. How do you actually run the place afterward, day to day, for decades? Spain's answer was a system called the forced-labor grant, or encomienda. It gave a Spanish colonist the legal right to demand labor and tribute from a Native community. In theory, that colonist owed something back in return. He owed protection from outside threats. He owed religious instruction in Christianity. In practice, encomienda holders very often worked people to death. They cared little for the promised protection. This was especially true in the silver mines that would soon make New Spain hugely profitable for Spain. The word encomienda comes from a Spanish verb meaning "to entrust." It sounds as if the Native community had simply been placed in a colonist's caring hands. The word and the actual reality did not always match. This same encomienda system later spread across Spain's growing American empire. It appeared wherever Spanish conquest reached a settled Native population large enough to exploit.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

encomienda (en-koh-mee-EN-dah) — a Spanish colonial system that gave a colonist the right to demand forced labor and tribute from Native peoples in a district, supposedly in exchange for protection and religious teaching.

A Debate Spain Itself Could Not Ignore

The cruelty of the encomienda system did not go unchallenged, even inside Spain. A Dominican friar named Bartolomé de las Casas became its fiercest critic. He had once held an encomienda of his own. That makes his change of heart especially striking. He witnessed the abuses firsthand. He wrote detailed, horrified accounts of what he saw happen to Native workers. He pushed the Spanish crown directly for reform. He traveled back to Spain to make his case in person. His tireless efforts led to real, if incomplete, changes in Spanish colonial law over the following decades. This matters for how you think about this whole period. Spain's American empire was not one single policy carried out smoothly. It held real moral disagreement. That disagreement was argued out loudly and in public, with real consequences. The disagreement never fully stopped the abuses it targeted. But it was genuinely real all the same, not just for show.

This disagreement eventually reached the highest level of the Spanish court. In 1550 and 1551, King Charles V called for a formal public debate. It took place in the city of Valladolid, in Spain. Las Casas argued that Native peoples were fully rational human beings. He said they deserved the same basic rights as any European. A rival scholar named Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda argued the opposite. He said some peoples were naturally suited to be ruled by others. He used ancient philosophical arguments to make his case. No official winner was ever declared. But the debate itself remains remarkable to historians today. Few, if any, other European empires held a public argument this early over whether the people they conquered counted as fully human.

Las Casas's advocacy did not stop at debate and published writing. In 1542, King Charles V issued a sweeping set of reforms called the New Laws. They aimed to phase out the encomienda system over time. They also aimed to end the practice of passing forced-labor grants down to a colonist's own children. Colonists in the Americas resisted these reforms fiercely. Their whole fortunes often depended on the labor encomienda guaranteed them. Some of the toughest New Laws provisions were softened or quietly reversed within a few years. But the New Laws still show something worth remembering. The long argument over encomienda produced real legal action from the highest levels of government. It was not just heartfelt words left sitting on a page.

⚠️ Watch Out
It is easy to read this lesson and decide that every Spaniard either wanted to destroy Native peoples or wanted to protect them, with nothing in between. Most people's real motives were more mixed and more ordinary than that. Profit, religious duty, ambition, and sometimes a genuine desire to reform things: these motives often sat uncomfortably together in the same person, or in the same royal court. A colonist could genuinely believe he was fulfilling his duty to protect and convert Native workers, while also driving them mercilessly in a silver mine, without feeling like a hypocrite at the time.

Building a Government, Not Just Winning a War

Winning a war and running a government are two very different jobs. Spain had to do both, one right after the other. In 1535, Spain created the Viceroyalty of New Spain. This was a new government covering Mexico and the surrounding conquered territory. A viceroy, appointed directly by the king, ruled from Mexico City in the king's own name. Beneath the viceroy, a network of Spanish officials, royal courts, and Catholic missions carried royal authority outward. It spread from the very city Cortés had destroyed and then rebuilt as his own capital. This new structure gave Spain something the old Aztec Empire had lacked. It had a single, clear chain of command answering to one distant king. The empire had been a loose network of separately tribute-paying city-states instead. Did this more centralized structure treat ordinary people any better? That is a separate question. This lesson has already begun to answer it, through the encomienda system's own brutal record.

Two Accounts, Two Positions

You already read Bernal Díaz's firsthand account of first seeing Tenochtitlan, in yesterday's primary source. He wrote it as an ordinary common soldier. He looked back on events that happened decades earlier in his own life. Next you will read Hernán Cortés's own words, from his Second Letter to King Charles V. Cortés wrote this letter in 1520, while the campaign was still underway. Its final outcome was still genuinely uncertain. He badly needed the king's approval. He had taken a whole series of actions without his own governor's permission. That included his unauthorized landing. That included his defiance of Velázquez's orders. A letter written to win royal favor reads differently than a memoir written decades later to settle old scores. This is true even when both documents describe the same city, the same market, and the same empire. Read Cortés's letter watching for what he chooses to emphasize, and what he leaves out. He is, in effect, asking a king to forgive his outright insubordination. His enormous gamble paid off. Charles V confirmed him as governor of New Spain, the very authority Governor Velázquez had refused to give him in the first place.
