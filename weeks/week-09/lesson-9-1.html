📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
A few words below may be new to you. Look them over before you read; they will also show up, underlined, in tomorrow's lesson. Tap the speaker to hear each word.

skirmish (SKUR-mish) — a small, brief fight between limited numbers of soldiers, usually smaller and shorter than a full battle.

ultimatum (ul-tuh-MAY-tum) — a final, formal demand that leaves no room for further negotiation, usually threatening serious consequences if it is refused.

📖 Today's Focus
Three parties claimed the Ohio Country in the 1750s. Only two of them were European.

By the 1750s, the Ohio River Valley sat at the meeting point of claims that could not all be true at once. France considered the valley part of a connected fur-trading network linking Canada to Louisiana along the Mississippi River. Virginia's colonial government, backing land speculators in the Ohio Company, considered the same valley open for British settlement under its own colonial charter. And the Ohio Country's actual inhabitants, including the Shawnee, the Delaware (also called the Lenape), Mingo settlers, and others, along with the more distant but still-influential Iroquois Confederacy, had their own interests in the territory. Neither European claim had any real right to override those interests, whatever each power's paperwork said.

The Iroquois Confederacy's involvement ran through a long-standing diplomatic relationship with the British colonies called the Covenant Chain, a series of alliances and agreements renewed over decades. British officials often treated the Confederacy as though it could speak for every Native nation in the Ohio Country, including the Shawnee and Delaware, who did not always agree that the Iroquois had any right to make decisions on their behalf. That mismatch, one power's convenient assumption about who could speak for whom, would matter again and again in the years ahead.

A Young Officer's Mission

In late 1753, Virginia's Governor Robert Dinwiddie sent 21-year-old Major George Washington, a young officer with no combat experience, to deliver a formal ultimatum demanding that French forces withdraw from the Ohio Country. The mission failed diplomatically. The French politely refused. But it gave Washington firsthand knowledge of the region's geography and its Native leadership, including a Mingo leader the English called the Half King (Tanaghrisson), who had his own reasons, independent of British interests, for wanting to check France's growing influence over the region's Native nations.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Tanaghrisson (tan-uh-GRIS-un) — a Mingo leader whom the English called the Half King, a title reflecting his role as a go-between among the Mingo, the Iroquois Confederacy, and British colonial officials. He had his own political goals, independent of British wishes, and his actions at Jumonville Glen shaped the war's opening chapter.

Jumonville Glen: The Skirmish That Started a War

In May 1754, Washington led a small force back into the Ohio Country and ambushed a French scouting party at a site called Jumonville Glen. The skirmish killed several soldiers, including the French commanding officer, Joseph Coulon de Jumonville. Accounts differ on exactly what happened next. Some sources indicate that Tanaghrisson himself killed the already-wounded Jumonville during the confused aftermath, for his own political reasons tied to Mingo relations with the French, not on any order from Washington. Whatever exactly occurred, French authorities treated the killing as murder rather than a legitimate act of war, and the incident escalated rapidly. Washington's small, hastily built post, Fort Necessity, fell to French and Native forces the following July, forcing his surrender. It was a humiliating start to a military career that would, decades later, culminate very differently.

💡 Helpful to Know
Notice how much of this war's opening chapter turns on the specific decisions and relationships of a handful of individuals: a young, inexperienced Virginia officer, a Mingo leader pursuing his own diplomatic strategy, a French officer killed in disputed circumstances. None of these people were trying to start a global war. The Seven Years' War that followed was not planned by anyone at Jumonville Glen; it grew out of a chain of specific, contingent decisions that could plausibly have gone differently at almost every step.
