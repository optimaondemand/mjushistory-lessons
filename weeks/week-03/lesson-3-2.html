📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Two new words below, plus one carried over from yesterday. Look them over before you read. Tap the speaker to hear each word.

confederacy (kun-FED-er-uh-see) — a group of separate nations or states that join together for shared purposes, such as defense or trade, while each keeps its own leaders.

missionary (MISH-un-air-ee) — a person sent by a church to teach its religion to people who do not already hold it.

pelt (PELT) — an animal skin with the fur still attached, traded as a raw material.

📖 Today's Focus
The beaver hat was, for a time, one of the most politically important objects in the world.

In Europe, felt hats made from beaver fur stayed in high demand for over a century. Wealthy men across the continent wore them as a mark of status. That steady demand built an enormous trade network. It reached from Paris all the way to the Great Lakes. European fashion connected directly to the deep interior of North America.

Beaver fur made unusually good felt, better than most other furs. Oddly enough, pelts that Native trappers had already worn and softened made the best felt of all. European buyers came to prize these worn pelts highly. Demand stayed high and steady for decades. But beaver do not breed fast enough to survive that kind of hunting pressure. Within a few decades, whole regions of the Northeast had been trapped nearly empty of beaver. Traders had to look elsewhere.

That scarcity gave the fur trade a kind of ticking clock. Once a region ran out of beaver, the whole trade had to move further on. French traders pushed steadily west, chasing the animals that remained. So did the Native nations who now depended on this trade. It had become their main source of valuable European goods: metal knives and kettles, woven cloth, and firearms. Nobody planned this westward expansion in advance. The animals simply ran out in one place after another. Everyone involved, French and Native alike, followed the beaver that were left.

The Huron, the Iroquois, and the Beaver Wars

The Iroquois Confederacy and the Huron Confederacy were rival powers long before any European arrived. The Iroquois Confederacy joined five nations under one shared structure: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca. The Huron people were organized as a confederacy of their own too, further north. Their rivalry with the Iroquois was already old. It began long before either side had heard of France.

The fur trade did not create this old rivalry out of nothing. What it did was sharpen it into something far more destructive. Historians call the result the Beaver Wars. This was a long, grinding struggle running through most of the 1600s. Both sides fought bitterly over who would control the richest fur-trapping territory. Whoever won that territory would receive the most valuable European trade goods. Firearms raised enormously what each side stood to win, and to lose, in every battle.

The results of this long struggle were severe and decisive. In 1649, large Iroquois war parties broke the Huron Confederacy apart. Huron survivors scattered in every direction, west and north. Some joined other neighboring nations for protection. Some fled to French settlements for shelter. This same confederacy had been one of the strongest powers in the region just one generation earlier. Now it ceased to exist as an independent people. France lost its most important Native ally in the space of one brutal season. Notice what this means for France's whole colonial strategy. The nation France had bet its entire fur trade on was destroyed. And it was destroyed by other Native people, using weapons that European trade itself had put into their hands.

France's alliance with the Huron carried a consequence France may not have fully thought through. You read earlier this week about Champlain's 1609 battle, beside a lake in what is now upstate New York. That single battle helped seal France's alliance with the Huron. It helped Huron warriors kill several Iroquois chiefs, in front of their own people. From the Iroquois point of view, that battle made France an enemy from that point on. And an enemy's enemy is always a possible future friend. Over the following decades, the Iroquois Confederacy moved steadily closer to France's rivals. First toward the Dutch, and later toward the English. Those alignments outlasted every person who first made them. They passed down through generations of leaders. They still mattered a full century later, during the French and Indian War, which you will study later this quarter. One fight beside one lake in 1609 helped quietly set the sides for a much larger war in the 1750s. That connection is worth sitting with for a moment.

Missionaries Follow the Trade

French religious orders followed the fur trade's own routes west. The most important of these was the Catholic order known as the Jesuits. Jesuit missionaries traveled to New France to convert Native peoples to Catholicism. Unlike many earlier European visitors, they came intending to stay. Many lived for years among the Huron and other Native nations, fully immersed in daily life there. They learned the local languages well enough to preach and hold real conversations. They ate the same food. They traveled by canoe alongside their hosts. They endured brutal winters they had never been raised for.

What grew out of these long, close encounters was genuinely complicated on both sides. In some cases, real friendship developed between missionaries and the Native families who hosted them. In other cases, there was deep, painful misunderstanding between two very different worldviews. Both the Huron and the Jesuits often held sincere religious conviction. That conviction sometimes pointed each side in opposite directions.

Every year, missionaries in the field sent long reports back to their superiors in France. These reports described their work and their observations. Published together, these documents are known as The Jesuit Relations. They stand today among the richest surviving records of Native life in this period. They also reveal French religious and cultural attitudes toward it. But read them the way you have learned to read every source in this course. They were written for a particular audience, and for a particular purpose. The mission needed steady financial support from readers back in France. A report describing no progress, year after year, would not bring in that money. None of this makes the Relations worthless as historical evidence. It simply makes them evidence that must be handled with real care, true of every historical source you will read.
