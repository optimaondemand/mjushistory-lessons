confederacy (kun-FED-er-uh-see) — a group of separate nations or states that join together for shared purposes, such as defense or trade, while each keeps its own leaders.

missionary (MISH-un-air-ee) — a person sent by a church to teach its religion to people who do not already hold it.

pelt (PELT) — an animal skin with the fur still attached, traded as a raw material.

📖 Today's Focus
The beaver hat was, for a time, one of the most politically important objects in the world.

In Europe, felt hats made from beaver fur stayed in high demand for well over a century. That demand built a trade network reaching from Paris all the way to the Great Lakes.

Beaver fur made unusually good felt. Oddly enough, pelts that had already been worn and softened by Native use made the best felt of all. Demand stayed high and steady for decades. But beaver do not breed fast enough to survive that kind of pressure. Within a few decades of the trade beginning, whole regions of the Northeast had been trapped nearly empty of beaver.

That gave the fur trade a built-in clock. Once a region ran out, the trade had to move. French traders pushed further west and deeper into the interior. So did the Native nations who now depended on the trade, because it was their source of European goods: metal knives and kettles, cloth, and firearms. Nobody sat down and planned this expansion. The animals ran out, and everyone involved followed the beaver that were left.

The Huron, the Iroquois, and the Beaver Wars

The Iroquois Confederacy and the Huron Confederacy were rival powers long before any European arrived. The Iroquois Confederacy joined five nations: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca. The Huron were a confederacy as well. Their rivalry was already old.

The fur trade did not create that rivalry. It sharpened it into something far more destructive. Historians call the result the Beaver Wars: a struggle running through most of the 1600s over who would control fur-trapping territory, and therefore who would receive European goods. Firearms raised what each side stood to win and to lose.

The results were severe. In 1649, Iroquois war parties broke the Huron Confederacy apart. Huron survivors scattered west and north, some joining other nations, some fleeing to the French settlements for shelter. A confederacy that had been one of the strongest powers in the region a generation earlier effectively ceased to exist as an independent people. France lost its most important ally in a single season. Notice what this means: the nation France had bet on was destroyed, and destroyed by other Native people using weapons that European trade had put into their hands.

France's decision to ally with the Huron carried a consequence France may not have thought all the way through. Champlain's 1609 battle, which you read about this week, helped seal that alliance by helping the Huron kill Iroquois chiefs. From the Iroquois point of view, that made France an enemy. And an enemy's enemy is a possible friend. Over the following decades the Iroquois moved toward France's rivals, first the Dutch and then the English. Those alignments outlasted every person who made them. They still mattered a century later in the French and Indian War, which you will study later this quarter. One fight beside one lake in 1609 helped set the sides for a war in the 1750s. That is worth sitting with for a moment.

Missionaries Follow the Trade

French religious orders followed the fur routes west, above all the Jesuits. Jesuit missionaries came to convert Native peoples to Catholicism, and they came to stay. Many lived for years among the Huron and other nations. They learned the languages. They ate the food, traveled by canoe, and endured winters they had not been raised for.

What grew out of that was complicated on both sides: real friendship in some cases, deep misunderstanding in others, and sincere religious conviction held by Huron and Jesuit alike, pointing in opposite directions.

Every year the missionaries sent long reports back to their superiors in France. Published together, these are known as The Jesuit Relations. They are among the richest surviving records we have of Native life in this period, and of French attitudes toward it. But read them the way you have learned to read every source in this course. They were written for a particular audience and a particular purpose: the mission needed money from readers in France, and a report showing no progress would not bring it in. That does not make the Relations worthless. It makes them evidence that has to be handled with care, which is true of all evidence.
