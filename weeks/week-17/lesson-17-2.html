📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Two new words below, plus two carried over from yesterday.

bicameral (by-KAM-er-ul) — having two separate lawmaking chambers, or houses, such as the House of Representatives and the Senate.

delegate (DEL-uh-git) — a person chosen to represent and act on behalf of a larger group at a meeting.

foreclosure (for-KLOH-zher) — when a lender takes away a property because the owner failed to pay what they owed.

ratify (RAT-uh-fy) — to officially approve a formal document, making it legally valid.

📖 Today's Focus
Delegates arrived in Philadelphia authorized only to revise the Articles of Confederation. Within weeks, they were writing an entirely new government from scratch instead.

Not every state took part. Rhode Island refused to send any delegates at all. It feared a stronger national government would threaten its own independence and interests. Fifty-five delegates represented the other twelve states instead. George Washington presided over the convention. His presence lent it enormous public credibility, even though he said little during the actual debates. Benjamin Franklin, then 81 years old, was the convention's oldest delegate. He was also one of its most respected voices, even as his own health steadily declined throughout that long summer. Thomas Jefferson and John Adams were both serving as diplomats in Europe at the time. Neither took any direct part in the drafting, a fact worth remembering whenever you picture "the founders" as one single unified room working from one single unified script, with every famous name from this quarter present in it.

James Madison of Virginia arrived having done more preparation than almost anyone else present. He had studied the history of failed confederacies. He did this specifically to design something more durable. His Virginia Plan proposed a strong national government. It based representation in both houses of a legislature on state population. That structure obviously favored large states like Virginia. Smaller states countered with the New Jersey Plan. It preserved the Articles' equal representation, regardless of a state's population.

The delegates conducted their work in complete secrecy. Windows were kept shut against the summer heat. This was meant specifically to prevent eavesdroppers from hearing the debates outside. Delegates also agreed not to discuss proceedings outside the room at all. Madison kept the most complete private notes of the debates. He did not allow them to be published until after his own death, decades later. Much of what historians know about the Convention's internal arguments comes from that single, delayed source.

The Great Compromise

The Connecticut Compromise, often called the Great Compromise, resolved this specific deadlock. It created a bicameral legislature. The House of Representatives would be apportioned by state population. This satisfied large states. The Senate would have equal representation for every state, regardless of size. This satisfied small states. This structural compromise, more than any single argument, is what actually allowed the convention to keep functioning. The large-state versus small-state conflict had threatened to break the whole convention apart entirely.

The Three-Fifths Compromise

A separate, morally weightier conflict concerned how slaves would count toward a state's population. This mattered for both representation and taxation. Southern states had large slave populations. They wanted slaves counted fully for representation. That would increase Southern political power. But they did not want slaves counted fully for taxation. Some Northern states wanted the reverse arrangement. The delegates settled on the Three-Fifths Compromise. Slaves would count as three-fifths of a person, for both representation and direct taxation purposes. This compromise was not a side issue or a footnote to the Convention's real business. It directly shaped how much political power slaveholding states held in the new government, for decades afterward. It is impossible to honestly separate two things here. One is the practical political bargain that made ratification possible. The other is the moral fact that this bargain treated slaves as fractional persons under the nation's founding law.

The Convention settled a third major disagreement, almost as contentious as the first two. This one concerned how to choose a president. Some delegates wanted Congress to select the executive directly. Others wanted a direct popular vote nationwide. The compromise that emerged was the Electoral College. This was a body of electors chosen by each state. Each state's number of electors was tied directly to its total representation in Congress, combining both its House seats and its two Senate seats. Like the Great Compromise, this split the difference between two camps that could not agree. It did not fully satisfy either one.

The delegates built in one more significant change. The Constitution itself specified it would take effect once nine of the thirteen states ratified it, not all thirteen. This broke from the Articles' own unanimity requirement, even before the ink was dry. Special state ratifying conventions, not the existing state legislatures, would vote on approval. This let supporters appeal directly to a broader, specially chosen body of voters in each state, gathered for this one purpose alone. It bypassed legislators who might have had reasons of their own to resist a stronger national government. The strategy worked. Ratifying conventions in state after state debated the document at length, and eventually approved it, though several state conventions came far closer to rejecting it outright than popular memory usually suggests today, decades and centuries removed from the actual vote.

⚠️ Watch Out
Do not let the Great Compromise's relatively tidy structural resolution (large states versus small states) lead you to expect an equally tidy resolution to the Three-Fifths Compromise. The Convention also protected the international slave trade from federal prohibition, until at least 1808. This was a further concession, made to secure Southern states' participation in the new union. These were not abstract debates. They were concrete political trades, with real, lasting human consequences. This course will keep tracking those consequences into the sectional crisis and Civil War, later in your study of U.S. History.
