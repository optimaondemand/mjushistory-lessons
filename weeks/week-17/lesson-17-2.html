📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary
Two new words below, plus two carried over from yesterday.

bicameral (by-KAM-er-ul) — having two separate lawmaking chambers, or houses, such as the House of Representatives and the Senate.

delegate (DEL-uh-git) — a person chosen to represent and act on behalf of a larger group at a meeting.

foreclosure (for-KLOH-zher) — when a lender takes away a property because the owner failed to pay what they owed.

ratify (RAT-uh-fy) — to officially approve a formal document, making it legally valid.

📖 Today's Focus
Delegates arrived in Philadelphia authorized only to revise the Articles. Within weeks, they were writing an entirely new government from scratch.

Fifty-five delegates convened in Philadelphia in May 1787. James Madison of Virginia arrived having done more preparation than almost anyone else present. He had studied the history of failed confederacies, specifically to design something more durable. His Virginia Plan proposed a strong national government. It based representation in both houses of a legislature on state population, a structure that obviously favored large states like Virginia. Smaller states countered with the New Jersey Plan, preserving the Articles' equal representation regardless of population.

The delegates conducted their work in complete secrecy. Windows were kept shut against the summer heat, specifically to prevent eavesdroppers from hearing the debates, and delegates agreed not to discuss proceedings outside the room. Madison kept the most complete private notes of the debates. He did not allow them to be published until after his own death, decades later. Much of what historians know about the Convention's internal arguments comes from that single, delayed source.

The Great Compromise

The Connecticut Compromise, often called the Great Compromise, resolved this specific deadlock. It created a bicameral legislature. The House of Representatives would be apportioned by state population, satisfying large states. The Senate would have equal representation for every state regardless of size, satisfying small states. This structural compromise, more than any single argument, is what actually allowed the convention to keep functioning. The large-state/small-state conflict had threatened to break it apart entirely.

The Three-Fifths Compromise

A separate, morally weightier conflict concerned how slaves would count toward a state's population for purposes of representation and taxation. Southern states had large slave populations. They wanted slaves counted fully for representation, increasing Southern political power, but not for taxation. Some Northern states wanted the reverse. The delegates settled on the Three-Fifths Compromise: slaves would count as three-fifths of a person for both representation and direct taxation purposes. This compromise was not a side issue or a footnote to the Convention's real business. It directly shaped how much political power slaveholding states held in the new government, for decades afterward. It is impossible to honestly separate two things here. One is the practical political bargain that made ratification possible. The other is the moral fact that this bargain treated slaves as fractional persons under the nation's founding law.

The Convention settled a third major disagreement almost as contentious as the first two: how to choose a president. Some delegates wanted Congress to select the executive directly. Others wanted a direct popular vote nationwide. The compromise that emerged was the Electoral College, a body of electors chosen by each state, in a number tied to that state's total representation in Congress. Like the Great Compromise, it split the difference between two camps that could not agree, rather than fully satisfying either one.

Convention Compromise Builder
Choose a delegate position, read the opposing side's own argument, then reveal the compromise that resolved the conflict.

⚠️ Watch Out
Do not let the Great Compromise's relatively tidy structural resolution (large states versus small states) lead you to expect an equally tidy resolution to the Three-Fifths Compromise. The Convention also protected the international slave trade from federal prohibition, until at least 1808. This was a further concession, made to secure Southern states' participation in the new union. These were not abstract debates. They were concrete political trades, with real, lasting human consequences. This course will keep tracking those consequences into the sectional crisis and Civil War, later in your study of U.S. History.
